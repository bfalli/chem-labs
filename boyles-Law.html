Boyle’s Law: pressure–volume lab

A syringe of air is sealed to a gas pressure sensor at room temperature. Move the plunger, record the pressure at each volume, and find the pattern.

Drag the plunger, or use the slider. Line up the front edge of the black rubber seal with the scale.

Hold the plunger still until the sensor reads Steady, then keep the point. The syringe scale is not the whole story: some air is also inside the sensor, so work out the real gas volume before you type it in.
Procedure
  1. Start with the syringe reading at 10.0 mL (it is already set). The air is now sealed in.
  2. Move the plunger to 5.0 mL and wait for a steady pressure.
  3. Enter the total gas volume, then press Keep point.
  4. Repeat at 10.0, 12.5, 15.0, 17.5, and 20.0 mL.
  5. Draw your graph on your lab report. Press Show curve fit and record the equation and R² with it, then answer the questions.
Teacher options

Each new session uses a slightly different room pressure, so no two students get identical numbers.

Questions

  1. Before collecting data: predict what happens to the pressure if you squeeze the air into half the volume. Explain it using what gas particles are doing.
  2. Is the relationship direct or inverse? Defend your choice using the shape of your pressure vs. volume graph and the exponent b in your power fit.
  3. Pick two data points where one gas volume is about double the other. Compare their pressures and show the numbers.
  4. Which factors must stay constant for your results to hold?
  5. Your syringe cannot be set to every volume. Use your fit equation to predict the pressure when the gas volume is 40.0 mL, and again when it is 2.5 mL. Show your work. Which prediction would you trust less, and why?
  6. The pattern you found holds for any trapped gas at constant temperature, not just a syringe. A free diver fills her lungs with 6.0 L of air at the surface, where the pressure is 1.0 atm. She dives until the pressure on her lungs is 4.0 atm, far more than your syringe ever reached. (a) What volume do her lungs now hold? Show your work. (b) What would happen to the air in her lungs if she held her breath while swimming back up to the surface?
